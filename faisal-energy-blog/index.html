From Gas to Sunlight

Can solar photovoltaic power and battery storage support Bahrain’s sustainable energy future?

Blog entry 1

Why SDG 7 matters

Electricity is easy to take for granted when lights, cooling systems and phones work normally. The harder question is what happens before electricity reaches the socket: which fuel is used, how much it costs over time, how reliable it is, and what environmental damage is created.

This project uses United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 7, which calls for affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern energy for all. The goal is broader than simply adding renewable technology. A system that is clean but unreliable cannot support homes, hospitals or businesses. A system that is reliable but unaffordable is also not sustainable.

The regional case is Bahrain. The current system is represented by natural-gas combined-cycle generation. The future alternative is solar photovoltaic power supported by battery storage, energy efficiency and a more flexible electricity grid.

Main question: Can solar PV with battery storage meet a growing share of Bahrain’s electricity needs more sustainably than continued dependence on natural gas?

My judgement is that solar and storage should become the main direction for new electricity investment, while efficient gas plants remain a transition and reliability resource. The decision is based on technical performance, lifetime economics, environmental impact and social fairness.

Framework: United Nations SDG 7 targets and indicators.

Targets, indicators and the energy challenge

SDG 7 has several connected targets. Target 7.1 focuses on universal access to affordable, reliable and modern energy. Target 7.2 calls for a substantial increase in renewable energy. Target 7.3 calls for faster improvement in energy efficiency. Targets 7.a and 7.b cover clean-energy cooperation, finance, technology and infrastructure.

The global challenge has two sides. Hundreds of millions of people still lack basic electricity, while countries with established systems must reduce emissions without weakening reliability.

Bahrain mainly faces the second challenge. It has modern electricity access, but its energy system is heavily dependent on fossil fuels. Hot weather creates high cooling demand, and desalination adds another important electricity requirement. Bahrain has committed to reducing emissions by 30% by 2035 and reaching net zero by 2060. Its national energy strategy focuses on efficiency, diversification and decarbonisation.

The regional problem is therefore clear: Bahrain must keep electricity dependable during summer peaks while reducing the carbon intensity of each kilowatt-hour.

Blog entry 3

The current system: natural-gas combined-cycle power

A combined-cycle gas turbine plant produces electricity in two stages. Natural gas is burned in a gas turbine, and the hot exhaust is used to produce steam for a second turbine. Using waste heat makes the plant more efficient than a simple-cycle gas turbine.

Technical strengths

The main advantage is dispatchability. Operators can increase or decrease output to match demand. This matters when air-conditioning demand remains high after sunset, when solar production has fallen. CCGT technology is mature, compact and supported by established engineering experience.

Economic strengths and risks

Existing gas infrastructure can reduce short-term investment pressure. However, fuel must be bought and burned throughout the plant’s life. This exposes electricity costs to gas availability, fuel prices and future carbon restrictions. A plant that looks inexpensive at construction can become costly when decades of fuel use are included.

Environmental impact

Natural gas produces less carbon dioxide than coal for the same electricity output, but it is not low-carbon. Combustion releases CO₂, and the upstream supply chain can leak methane.

Blog entry 4

The future alternative: solar PV with battery storage

Solar PV converts sunlight directly into electricity. An inverter changes the direct-current output into alternating current for buildings or the grid. Battery storage does not generate energy; it stores electricity and releases it later. This is important because demand continues into the evening after solar output has fallen.

Why it suits Bahrain

Bahrain has a strong solar resource and opportunities beyond large desert sites. Rooftops, industrial areas, car-park canopies and previously developed land can support solar deployment while reducing land conflict. Solar production also overlaps with part of the daytime cooling load.

Technical limitations

Solar output varies with the time of day, clouds, dust and high panel temperature. Batteries improve reliability but have finite duration, efficiency losses, cost and material requirements. A high-renewable grid also needs forecasting, demand management, stronger networks and some dispatchable backup.

Economic direction

IRENA reports that the global weighted-average levelised cost of utility-scale solar PV commissioned in 2024 was about USD 0.043 per kWh. Project costs differ by country, financing and storage needs, but the trend shows why solar is now a mainstream power option.

Blog entry 5

Which system is more sustainable?

CriterionNatural-gas CCGTSolar PV + battery
ControllabilityStrong; output can be scheduled around demand.Solar is variable; batteries and grid flexibility improve control.
FuelContinuous gas supply is required.No fuel cost after installation.
EconomicsExisting assets may be economical, but fuel creates ongoing cost and risk.Higher upfront investment, low operating cost and no fuel bill.
Life-cycle emissionsAbout 490 gCO₂e/kWh in this comparison.About 48 gCO₂e/kWh in this comparison.
Land and materialsCompact plant site, but extraction and pipelines have impacts.More surface area and mineral use; rooftops and recycling reduce impacts.
Energy securityDepends on long-term fuel supply.Uses domestic sunlight but needs imported technology and materials.

Technically, gas is stronger for controllable generation today. Solar and batteries become stronger as storage duration, forecasting and grid flexibility improve. Economically, gas carries fuel and carbon risk, while solar concentrates more of its cost at the start.

Environmentally, solar has the clear advantage. Gas creates emissions whenever it operates. Solar PV has manufacturing and end-of-life impacts, but operational emissions are very low. Responsible sourcing, recycling and careful siting are still required.

Final judgement: solar PV supported by battery storage is the more sustainable option for Bahrain’s future electricity growth. Natural gas should remain a transition and reliability resource while solar, storage, efficiency and grid flexibility expand.

Practical activity: household energy-audit demonstration

The practical activity is a small energy audit based on appliance nameplate ratings and realistic daily operating times. It demonstrates how demand is calculated and how efficiency changes the size of a clean-energy system. The calculation is transparent, so it can be repeated with another household’s appliances.

The audit gives about 532.2 kWh per month and 6,475 kWh per year. Air conditioning represents about 68% of the listed daily demand, so cooling efficiency is the first priority.

For an illustrative solar calculation, assume a 3.3 kWp PV system and an annual yield of 1,801.7 kWh per kWp:

3.3 × 1,801.7 = 5,945.6 kWh/year

Using the project’s emission factors, estimated annual avoided emissions are:

5,945.6 × (0.490 − 0.048) = 2,627.6 kgCO₂e/year

This equals approximately 2.63 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent avoided each year. The result also shows why efficiency matters. A more efficient air conditioner, better insulation and careful thermostat settings reduce both electricity use and the required solar-battery capacity.

Blog entry 7

Ethical and social dimensions

An energy transition changes more than equipment. It changes employment, prices, access to technology and the distribution of environmental harm. The ethical question is not only “Which system has lower emissions?” but also “Who benefits, who pays and who is protected during the change?”

Who benefits?

Households and businesses benefit from cleaner electricity, lower exposure to future fuel-price changes and improved energy security. Bahrain can benefit from new skills in solar engineering, battery management, efficient buildings and digital grid operation. Future generations benefit from lower climate risk.

Who may be affected?

Workers and suppliers connected to fossil fuels may face disruption if the transition is too sudden. Lower-income households may be excluded if efficient appliances or rooftop solar are available only to people who can afford a large upfront cost. Communities outside Bahrain may also carry mining and manufacturing impacts for batteries and PV materials.

Ethical issueResponsible response
AffordabilityProtect essential electricity use and design finance so benefits are not limited to wealthy households.
ReliabilityPhase the transition carefully and maintain sufficient backup, storage and grid capacity.
EmploymentOffer reskilling routes into solar, storage, efficiency and grid work.
MaterialsRequire responsible sourcing, repair, recycling and end-of-life management.
Public trustExplain benefits and limitations honestly rather than presenting any technology as perfect.

The ethical conclusion is simple: an electricity system is not truly sustainable if it lowers emissions while making access less fair. Bahrain’s transition should combine environmental improvement with reliability, affordability and worker protection.

Personal reflection

The main lesson from this project is that sustainability is a balance of engineering and society. Gas is dependable but carbon-intensive. Solar is low-carbon but variable. Batteries help, but they add cost and material impacts. The strongest pathway is a managed transition: reduce waste first, add solar and storage, strengthen the grid and retain enough flexible capacity to protect reliability.

The practical audit made the issue more concrete. It showed that clean generation is only one part of the solution. Reducing cooling demand can lower costs immediately and make the future energy system easier to build.
